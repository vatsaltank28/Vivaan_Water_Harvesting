Why switch to rainwater harvesting?

Rainwater harvesting is an exemplary method of water conservation and a recognised green practice. As population grows, demand for water rises exponentially. Much of that demand can be managed by collecting and storing rainwater.

Contrary to popular myth, RWH is economical, convenient and easy to manage. It also promotes infiltration into the subsoil, improving vegetation cover and agriculture.

03

Water Audit, Conservation & Water Neutrality

A water audit reviews domestic, sanitary, flushing, landscaping and process water use, and gives a roadmap to cut consumption or find new sources.

  • Audit all water-using equipment and processes
  • Find inefficient fixtures, leaks and waste
  • Check kitchen equipment and processes
  • Inspect pools, fountains and ponds for leaks
  • Check cooling-tower float valves
  • Review irrigation schedules and sprinklers
  • Compare meter data to catch metering errors
  • Conservation measures with savings, cost and payback

Water neutrality encourages water-consuming industries to reduce consumption and compensate for what remains by investing in projects that promote sustainable, equitable water use in the affected community. Water conservation methods are identified per site; harvested rainwater can recharge a borewell or ring well, or be stored in ponds and tanks.

04

Borewell & Hydrogeological Surveys

Hydrogeological survey. Investigation of underground formations to understand the hydrologic cycle, groundwater quality, and the nature, number and type of aquifers. We use the ERT (electrical resistivity tomography) method: current is induced in the ground through two electrodes to map subsurface strata such as soil, weathered and fractured layers, and the depth and geometry of bedrock. The results drive an effective recharge design.

Borewell survey. The dowsing method is used to locate points with water-yield potential, and the geologist recommends final drilling points.
